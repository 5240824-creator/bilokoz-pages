Core method · 2026/2027

MoM

MoM is not a technique or a set of exercises but a discipline in its own right: a school with its own theory, its own descriptive language and a training programme of eleven levels. A large community of practitioners has grown up around it, predominantly English-speaking. Its originator is Andrew Austin, a British psychotherapist, trainer, speaker, researcher and author. The method acquired its own name in 2009 and has been taught as a systematic course since 2012.

Where the method came from

Austin tells it this way: he did not invent the name himself — he was simply doing the thing without calling it anything.

“I didn’t know I was — this is what I was doing. It was Steve Andreas who pointed this out. Essentially, what’s happening is we are dealing predominantly in therapy with people with what is known as stuck states — there’s a lack of progress — and what we’re doing is we’re moving them from a position of being stuck to a position of movement.” Andrew Austin, MoM trainer course

The whole method sits inside that sentence: the work is not with the content of the problem but with being stuck — and the task is to return movement.

Two systems

Integral Eye Movement Therapy came first — MoM grew out of it. Today they are two distinct disciplines: they solve different problems, complement one another and work well together in a single session.

MoM

When the tools run out

The person understands what is happening but cannot do anything with it: there is no move available. The client draws the geography of the state the problem lives in — and changes it.

This is not metaphor interpretation. The metaphor is not decoded — what gets explored is the space in which it exists.

Integral Eye Movement Therapy

When the problem is bigger than the person

Integral Eye Movement Therapy is Austin’s earlier discipline. It is used when the issue is not a shortage of tools but the sheer force of the emotional response: that has to come down before anything can change.

Work with emotional imprints and identity patterns through eye movement. Related to EMDR in its principle of bilateral activation.

MoM — it’s all quite simple

Someone comes in and says: “I’m stuck.”

They might of course say anything at all, but let us assume they said precisely that. “Anything at all” can be worked with just as effectively.

It is a workable start. They have already told us what is happening to them. All that remains is to work out what it was they just told us.

Because by this point the practitioner usually has several good explanations ready. Overload. Fear of change. Difficulty deciding. Sometimes the explanation is ready before the person has got their coat off.

MoM requires an unexpected skill: not understanding someone conclusively before they have finished speaking — especially when the story sounds familiar.

There is rapport. And there is disrapport — not a typo. Sometimes the other person corrects you: “No. That is not what I meant at all.” For the work this is excellent news. It does not always show on the practitioner’s face.

Then comes the autogenous metaphor — the image the person brings in themselves. “I’m stuck.” We begin to ask about it, following the method’s protocol rather than our curiosity. Behind the simple questions sit a taxonomy of metaphorical structures and relational positions.

Gradually a map appears — one you can draw and check between you.

And it turns out that a wall is not yet a reason to look for a door. Particularly when the door has so far been thought up only by the practitioner.

So far, so simple.

Then we take an interest in coping strategies. And something curious emerges: there is a great deal of effort and noticeably less strategy. A person can repeat the same move for years, each time with fresh hope. And yes, psychology is rich in examples of determination in the direction of a wall.

The client is here partly because they have already heard the excellent suggestion to do it differently. Including from themselves.

Further into the work come Boundary Violations. Idiomising. Structure-dependent interventions — which have an order, conditions and prohibitions.

That last one is particularly inconvenient for lovers of technique: you have finally learnt to do something interesting, and now you must learn to notice when not to do it.

Advice is rarely given here. The movements can be very small, which mildly disappoints anyone who judges the work by the size of the gesture. But a big step remains a big step. That does not make it a good one.

Alongside sits Integral Eye Movement Therapy, which deals with what lived experience has left behind. Which of the two fits a given case is not decided by the name of the presenting problem.

There is kinaesthetic denial. A person may feel better during the session itself, and that is a fine thing. It simply does not follow that, because it felt better in the room, the problem has been notified accordingly and ceased to exist.

And sometimes we walk the Strange Road, where a person gives advice to someone other than themselves. Some strategies sound convincing right up to that moment.

Sometimes what the work needs from a client is a firm “No.” It can be worth more than polite agreement.

That, essentially, is all. A fairly simple method.

Give or take rapport, disrapport, mapping, relational positioning, Boundary Violations, coping strategies, idiomising, structure-dependent interventions, stuck states, patterns of chronicity, autogenous metaphors, the Hurts bias, secondary gain, axis deviations, transitional states, The Three Pillars, kinaesthetic denial, Strange Road, The Maybe Man and a good deal else — along with the small matter that all of it takes years to learn and practise, and to notice your own mistakes before they harden into a confident explanation of someone else’s life.

The client does not have to sit this exam.

It is enough to arrive with your own “I’m stuck.” To speak in ordinary words. To correct the practitioner. And to work out together what is still hard to describe.

A few words about Austin

Andrew Austin is British. Until he was around twenty-eight he worked as a nurse, and his last hospital post was in the neurosurgical department of a large teaching hospital: critically ill patients, coming round from anaesthesia, post-operative delirium. He also talked his way into psychiatry: he rang a secure unit himself, asked to come and get some experience — and left his job in order to be there every day. On his own place in neurosurgery he is brief: at first he was the lowest of the low.

His way of looking comes from the same place. The eye movements that Integral Eye Movement Therapy works with are, by his own account, something he first saw in neurosurgery.

After that came hypnotherapy and psychotherapy — and rapid success. A practice, a stream of clients, grateful testimonials, full technical virtuosity: Ericksonian language patterns, deep trance, striking phenomena.

And this is where the thing I regard as a professional act begins. Austin stopped being satisfied with his own success. He went and surveyed his former clients.

“The problem was, whilst I was really good, at the end of the session clients would report how they felt and what things were like for them. But the reality was, all I was doing was measuring kinaesthetic change. The person has come to see me, they’ve had a nice cup of tea, they’ve had a 20-minute trance, they’ve talked about their problems, they’ve had some attention from me, and yeah, they felt good. But the question was, had they actually changed?

So I surveyed them, and I’ve got to tell you, it wasn’t a particularly good day for me. Because the reports came back with one consistent feature. They all said, yeah, you are a very nice guy. But when I then follow them up and say, well, hang about, I know I’m a nice guy — is your problem any different? Well, we thought you were a nice guy.

People felt that I was good. They felt the techniques that I did were interesting. They felt the whole thing was kind of useful. At the end of the sessions they all felt very pleased. They seemed very, very grateful. They paid the bill, which was very good.

So I started to change what I was doing.” Andrew Austin, talk in Cochin

The picture that came back was this: for many of them nothing had changed. Which is, in fact, true of almost all of psychology — it is just that very few people take the trouble to check.

Most people stop at that point: the testimonials are good, after all. Austin started building a method. What he was looking for was not a way to be liked but a way to produce change that holds.

Time and place helped: the nineties and early two-thousands in Britain were a high point for applied psychology — real interest, a strong scene.

The intellectual foundations

Austin names openly those he stands on. Each played a different part — an idea, teaching, joint development, support.

Charles Faulkner
American researcher of metaphor. Austin calls him, alongside Laing, “the biggest influence on my work today”. Conversations with Faulkner gave the impetus for MoM; the formula the method rests on — worlds within a word — is his. Austin brought him over to speak in Britain, spent a day with him and, by his own admission, “didn’t understand a word he said”.
Steve & Connirae Andreas
A direct contribution to the model itself: the method’s own manual states that “significant contributions to the development of the MoM model have come from Steve, Connirae, and Mark Andreas”. The method was named by Steve Andreas — Austin took the name from Andreas’s written description of one of his early presentations: Andreas saw and named what Austin was doing before Austin knew he was doing it.
George Lakoff
The cognitive theory of metaphor. Austin describes him as a pioneer and one of the major influences on the development of MoM.
R. D. Laing
Scottish psychiatrist, a central figure of anti-psychiatry. The second “biggest influence”, level with Faulkner. Austin’s advice to students on Laing is short: buy everything he wrote.
Gregory Bateson
The double bind and the ecology of mind — the frame in which a problem stops being a property of the person and becomes a property of the system around them.
David Grove
Originator of Clean Language: work with the client’s metaphor without substituting your own. Austin says plainly that he does something different — but the inspiration comes from there.
James Lawley & Penny Tompkins
Symbolic Modelling. Austin: “an ideology I largely got from the clean language guys”.
Richard Bandler
Co-founder of NLP — the scene Austin came out of. He describes first meeting Farrelly as astonishing, because it was like talking to Bandler, who had in his time gone through a Farrelly phase of his own.
Frank Farrelly
Provocative therapy. Austin went to a training with him; that is where the habit of speaking to a client directly, without hedging, comes from. On Farrelly’s book his advice is not to think about it: just buy it.
Nick Kemp
Colleague and fellow experimenter: the two of them went to Farrelly together, and years later tested how much work fits into half an hour rather than a full one. It turned out: the same amount. Hence the short session.

The foundations are broad — and they show how integrated the method is: how many pillars, giants and genuine authorities became Austin’s teachers and inspirations. And this is far from the full list. The method itself is his own work, with more than thirty years of practice behind it: MoM acquired its name in 2009, Austin began developing Integral Eye Movement Therapy earlier still, and before both of them came the hypnotherapy practice — the one he set out to check. Today it is eleven levels of training, a school and a community around it.
